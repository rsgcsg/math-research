# Finite abelian unit-distance graphs, general Galois orbits, and boundary extension

Date: 2026-09-07. Project: Hadwiger--Nelson research.

## 0. Status, provenance, and what is actually resolved

The real-plane Hadwiger--Nelson problem is NOT resolved by this report. The public baseline checked against Haugland, arXiv:2608.04542v4 (17 August 2026), remains 5 <= chi(R^2) <= 7. No new non-five-colourable finite unit-distance graph and no whole-plane five- or six-colouring is supplied.

What is resolved here is a complete structural subproblem: which Cayley graphs of finite abelian groups have injective unit-edge representations in the Euclidean plane. They are exactly disjoint unions of isomorphic Cartesian products of cycles and copies of K2. This classifies every single real abelian Galois orbit, not merely the additively separated radical expressions treated in the preceding report. Mixed radical terms do not escape this classification.

The deductions are supplied with complete mathematical arguments. No literature priority claim or external peer-review claim is made. A targeted literature search located standard definitions, Cartesian-product/hypercube realization context, and Payan's theorem on abstract cubelike graphs, but did not establish whether this exact classification has appeared elsewhere. The proofs here do not assume novelty.

Starting resources: the uploaded `HN_quadratic_branch_separation_and_orbit_classification_2026-09-07.md` and its `HN_galois_branch_and_orbit_exact_package_2026-09-07.zip`. This run executed the preceding package's `verify.sh`: small exact examples and the listed-edge positive witness for the 12277-point graph passed. It did NOT rerun that package's exhaustive large all-pairs geometry or all earlier research packages. All distances in the five NEW small configurations below were independently recomputed with exact rational coefficient arithmetic.

The word 'plane' refers to Euclidean coordinates; crossing edges are allowed. Vertices must be distinct. A faithful graph includes every unit pair. The necessary part of the main theorem is stronger: it only assumes that every specified edge is unit, even if extra unit pairs are allowed.

## 1. The geometric lemma: every injectively drawn unit four-cycle is a rhombus

Let four distinct points a,b,c,d satisfy

    |a-b|=|b-c|=|c-d|=|d-a|=1.

Then

    a+c=b+d.

Indeed, a and c are distinct common points of the two distinct unit circles centred at b,d. Those two intersection points are symmetric about (b+d)/2. This proof includes every possible injective unit drawing; no assumption of noncrossing straight edges is added.

The injectivity and dimension hypotheses matter. Coincident opposite vertices would invalidate the conclusion. In dimension three, two spheres intersect in a circle, not just two points.

## 2. Complete classification of finite abelian Cayley graphs

Let A be a finite abelian group, written additively, and S=-S subset A\{0}. Let X=Cay(A,S). Choose one representative s_i from every inverse class {s,-s} of S. For order-two elements this class is a singleton. Put H=<S>.

**Theorem 1.** The following are equivalent.

1. There is an injective F:A -> R^2 with |F(x+s)-F(x)|=1 for all x in A and s in S.
2. The cyclic subgroups <s_i> form an internal direct product:

       H = direct_sum_i <s_i>.

3. Each component of X is a Cartesian product

       C_{n_1} square ... square C_{n_r} square K2 square ... square K2,

   where n_i>=3. Components are mutually isomorphic.

Moreover, every graph in (3) has a faithful planar unit-distance realization.

### 2.1 Proof of necessity

Write D_sF(x)=F(x+s)-F(x). If s,t belong to different inverse classes, x,x+s,x+s+t,x+t are four distinct vertices of a unit four-cycle. Section 1 gives

    D_sF(x+t)=D_sF(x).

Fix i and let B_i be the subgroup generated by all inverse classes except {s_i,-s_i}. Thus D_{s_i}F is invariant under every translation in B_i.

Suppose h=m s_i belongs to <s_i> intersect B_i, choosing m>=0. Telescoping along m copies of s_i shows that

    D_hF(x)=F(x+h)-F(x)

is also B_i-invariant. In particular it is invariant under translation by h. Hence for every positive integer q,

    F(x+qh)=F(x)+q D_hF(x).

Since h has finite order q_0, this implies q_0 D_hF(x)=0 in R^2, so F(x+h)=F(x). Injectivity gives h=0.

Therefore <s_i> intersect B_i={0} for every i, which makes the sum of the cyclic subgroups direct. This proves (1) => (2). The equivalence of (2) and (3) follows immediately from the Cayley adjacency rule. Notice that no spectral or numerical assertion enters the proof.

### 2.2 Proof of sufficiency, including faithfulness

For each cyclic factor C_n use the vertices of a regular n-gon scaled to have unit sides; for K2 use a unit segment. Independently rotate these finite planar factors and take all vector sums of one vertex from each factor.

Every product edge remains unit because it changes only one factor to an adjacent vertex. Generic choices of the finitely many rotation angles make all summed points distinct and prevent every additional unit pair. To justify 'generic', fix a pair of product vertices. If it differs in only one factor, its distance is either the prescribed unit side or a strictly longer polygon chord. If it differs in at least two factors, its squared distance is a nonconstant trigonometric polynomial in the independent rotation angles: at least one cross term has nonzero amplitude. Therefore neither collision nor accidental distance one is an identity in the angles. There are finitely many pairs, so avoiding their proper analytic zero sets is possible.

Place different connected components by generic translations to prevent cross-component unit pairs. This proves faithful realizability.

### 2.3 Geometry of every such realization

In a connected representation, the rhombus identities also imply

    F(k_1s_1+...+k_ts_t) = F(0) + sum_i [F(k_i s_i)-F(0)].

Thus the actual embedding splits into a vector sum of closed unit-edge polygonal factors. The abstract product is reflected in the coordinates; it is not just a colouring bound.

### 2.4 Exact chromatic number

The empty product has chromatic number one. A nontrivial product with only even cycles and K2 factors is bipartite. If an odd cycle factor occurs, the chromatic number is exactly three.

For the upper bound, colour each factor properly using elements of Z/3Z and colour a product vertex by the sum of its factor colours modulo three. An edge changes exactly one summand, hence changes the total. A factor embeds by fixing all other coordinates, giving the matching lower bound.

In particular every finite abelian Cayley graph admitting an injective planar unit representation has chromatic number at most three.

### 2.5 Finiteness cannot be dropped

The proof used that a displacement h has finite order. On an infinite group, the equation F(x+qh)=F(x)+qD_hF(x) is perfectly compatible with a nonzero drift.

For example the infinite triangular lattice is Cay(Z^2,{+/-e_1,+/-e_2,+/-(e_1-e_2)}), with dependent generators but an injective unit realization. More generally the historical finite-direction reduction of HN uses INFINITE, finitely generated abelian hosts. Theorem 1 does not bound those hosts by three and does not contradict the known five-colour examples embedded in them.

## 3. An exact extremal corollary within the classified class

For n>=1 let e_ab(n) be the maximum number of edges in an n-vertex finite abelian Cayley graph that has an injective planar unit representation. Write Omega(n) for the total number of prime factors, counting multiplicities, and v_2(n) for the multiplicity of 2; put both equal to zero at n=1.

**Corollary 2.**

    e_ab(n) = (n/2) [2 Omega(n)-v_2(n)].

Proof: a cycle factor of order m contributes degree two, except a factor K2 contributes one. These contributions are bounded by w(m)=2Omega(m)-v_2(m). The component order h divides n, so the degree is at most w(h)<=w(n). Attainment follows by taking one K2 factor for each prime factor 2, and one C_p factor for each odd prime factor p. Their product has n vertices, is faithfully unit-realizable, and has degree w(n).

A simpler uniform bound is

    e_ab(n) <= n log_3 n,

with equality for n=3^r, realized by Cartesian powers of C3. In the elementary abelian two-group case, a component is Q_t and its edge count is exactly t 2^(t-1). These are statements about the classified finite Cayley class, not bounds for arbitrary planar unit-distance graphs.

## 4. Every single real abelian Galois orbit is classified

Let K subset L subset R be a finite Galois extension with abelian group G. For z in L^2 take the distinct orbit

    O(z)={sigma(z):sigma in G}.

Automorphisms preserve squared-distance equations and fix 1. They also preserve non-equality to 1: sigma(q)=1 implies q=1. Consequently the full unit graph on O(z) is G-invariant.

The stabilizer B of z is normal because G is abelian. The action on the orbit is the regular action of A=G/B, so the unit graph is a Cayley graph of A. Theorem 1 applies.

**Corollary 3.** Every component of U(O(z)) is a Cartesian product of cycles and K2 factors. In particular chi<=3.

If G is an elementary abelian two-group, then all factors are K2:

    U(O(z)) = disjoint union of identical hypercubes,
    chi <= 2.

This holds for an arbitrary expression in the field, including mixed radical terms. It strictly removes the 'additively separated expression' assumption in the preceding report.

More generally, if G is any finite abelian two-group, all cyclic factors have even order, so the orbit is still bipartite, though its components need not be cubes.

Any SUBSET of an orbit satisfies the same colour bound, by restriction. Deleting unwanted conjugates cannot escape it.

Scope: this does not say that the full coordinate field is three-colourable. It says nothing comparable about arbitrary unions of many orbits. It also does not apply to a real point whose full normal orbit has nonreal points, or to nonabelian Galois actions. A real field here need only be real in the chosen relative setting; we require L/K to be finite Galois with all its automorphism images inside the same real field L.

## 5. Why one orbit sees a uniform boundary list

Fix a finite old point set P subset K^2 and a prescribed proper colouring c of P. Let O be the orbit of a genuinely new point z not in K^2. Every point of O has exactly the same old unit-neighbour set

    N={p in P:|z-p|=1}.

Indeed all p are fixed by every automorphism. Thus every orbit vertex has the SAME allowed colour set

    L_O={available colours}\c(N).

For |O|>=2, the circle intersection bound gives |N|<=2. If |O|>=3, it gives |N|<=1: two distinct old centres cannot have three distinct common unit neighbours.

If N is nonempty, the entire orbit lies on a unit circle centred at an old point. The unit-distance graph on a circle of radius one is bipartite: unit edges change the angle by +/-pi/3, so components are subgraphs of six-cycles. This assertion is about radius ONE, not every possible circle radius.

**Theorem 4 (one-orbit extension).** Under the real abelian hypotheses of Section 4, every prescribed proper four-colouring of P extends to P union O, and hence to P union any subset of O.

Proof: if N is empty, use the three-colour bound. If |N|=1, at least three colours remain and the orbit is bipartite. If |N|=2, O has exactly two vertices and at least two colours remain. All cases extend.

This is an extension theorem, stronger than merely saying the enlarged graph has some four-colouring.

## 6. Sharp four-colour extension threshold for at most two two-power orbits

**Theorem 5.** Let L/K be a finite real Galois extension whose group is an abelian two-group. Let P subset K^2 have a prescribed proper k-colouring, with k>=4. If all new points belong to at most TWO nontrivial Galois orbits, that SPECIFIC old colouring extends to all new points. The universal guarantee is sharp: it is false for k=3 even for a single orbit of size two.

It is enough to complete the two orbits O_1,O_2. They are disjoint unless identical, in which case Theorem 4 applies. Each is bipartite by Corollary 3 and has a uniform allowed palette L_i of size at least k-2.

### 6.1 A geometric matching lemma for anchored two-power orbits

If an orbit O has any old neighbour p, it lies on the radius-one circle about p. Its internal unit graph has maximum degree two, and every cycle is a six-cycle. The Galois action is transitive and preserves adjacency, so its internal degree is constant. Since |O| is a power of two, it cannot be a disjoint union of six-cycles. Therefore its internal graph has maximum degree at most ONE: it is a matching or an independent set.

If an orbit has at least two old neighbours, it has exactly two vertices. Those two old neighbours exhaust the intersection of the unit circles about the orbit vertices. Consequently NO genuinely new vertex can be adjacent to both members of this orbit.

If two orbits are both anchored, each new point sees at most two points on the other orbit's old-centred unit circle. The centre of that other circle is old, and cannot equal the new point, so the two-circle bound applies.

### 6.2 The four-colour case

If |L_1 union L_2|>=4, Hall's elementary two-set condition provides disjoint two-element subsets C_i subset L_i. Colour the two bipartite orbits from disjoint palettes; all cross edges are automatically proper.

Otherwise their union has size at most three, and each palette has size at least two. There are three cases.

(a) Both palettes have size three. They are identical. Each orbit is anchored and hence internally a matching. Each vertex has at most two cross-orbit neighbours, so the union graph has maximum degree at most three. A planar unit-distance graph contains no K4 (four distinct mutually equidistant points do not fit in R^2). Brooks' theorem therefore gives a proper colouring from the common three-colour palette. This is the one external graph-colouring theorem used in this extension argument; see reference [6]. Crossing edges remain allowed.

(b) One palette has size two, the other size three. The first orbit has only two vertices, and no new vertex is adjacent to both. Colour this pair properly from its palette. Every point of the other orbit loses at most one colour and still has at least two choices. Its internal graph is a matching, so it can be coloured independently edge by edge.

(c) Both palettes have size two. Both orbits have two vertices, and the cross edges form a matching. Together with their possible internal edges, the four-vertex union is a subgraph of an even cycle: every component is a path or C4. Such graphs are two-choosable, so the two possibly different palettes extend. For completeness, a path can be coloured greedily; an even cycle with identical two-lists is coloured alternately. If adjacent lists differ, choose at one endpoint a colour absent from the other's list, break the cycle at that edge, and greedily traverse the resulting path.

This proves the four-colour case.

### 6.3 Five or more colours

For k>=6 the palettes each have at least four elements, so disjoint two-element subsets exist. For k=5 they each have at least three. If their union has size at least four the same argument works. Otherwise they are the same three-element set and each orbit has exactly two vertices. Their four-vertex union is not K4 and so is three-colourable. Thus every k>=4 is covered WITHOUT recolouring any old point.

### 6.4 Sharpness at three colours

Take K=Q(sqrt2), L=K(sqrt3), old points

    p=(0,0), q=(1,sqrt2),

and new conjugates

    z_+ = (1/2-sqrt6/6, sqrt2/2+sqrt3/6),
    z_- = (1/2+sqrt6/6, sqrt2/2-sqrt3/6).

The full unit graph is K4 minus the old edge pq: both new points see both old points and are unit distance apart. Precolour p,q with distinct colours 0,1. With three colours both new points are forced to colour 2 and conflict. With four colours use 2 and 3. All six distances and all nine three-colour extension assignments are checked exactly. This is a classical diamond used as a calibration, not a new high-chromatic record.

Consequences: in this model, a failure of either four- or five-colour extension requires at least THREE new Galois orbits, or new points already in K^2, or departure from the real abelian two-extension hypotheses. Three orbits are only a necessary threshold, not a sufficient construction. Nothing here bounds an arbitrary union of many orbits by four.

## 7. Low ordinary colour number is not low list-colouring complexity

The orbit classification alone must not be used to discard arbitrary list constraints. The supplied rational planar realization of Q3 has two colours but admits the following bad two-list assignment, with vertices labelled 0,...,7 by bits:

    0,1,2,3 : {0,1}
    4,5     : {0,2}
    6,7     : {1,2}.

The first square has only two legal patterns: (0,1,1,0) or its reversal. In the first case the vertical constraints force vertices 4 and 6 both to colour 2, although they are adjacent. In the second, vertices 5 and 7 are forced to colour 2 and are adjacent. Thus no list-colouring exists.

All 256 assignments from the lists are checked independently. The graph has eight rational points and exactly twelve unit edges, all pair distances checked.

This does not contradict Theorems 4 and 5: those theorems use lists that are constant on each complete orbit, together with geometric restrictions when the lists shrink to size two. The arbitrary lists above are not generated by an old K-boundary on one complete new orbit.

## 8. Five exact small configurations

### 8.1 A genuinely mixed multiquadratic expression

In Q(sqrt2,sqrt3,sqrt19), put

    z=(sqrt114/3 + sqrt6/10 + sqrt2/4,
       sqrt114/5 + sqrt19/10 + sqrt2/4).

Take all eight conjugates. There are FOUR nonzero character components, with supports {1,3,4,7} in the radical basis indexed by the primes 2,3,19; these characters have rank three. Thus this is genuinely beyond a sum of independent single-character terms.

The actual graph consists of two disjoint squares: eight vertices and eight unit edges. With conjugations labelled by g in (F2)^3, its connection set is {3,6}. All 28 point-pair distances are checked exactly.

### 8.2 A planar faithful Q3 with a bad list assignment

Take all subset sums of

    (1,0), (3/5,4/5), (5/13,12/13).

There are exactly eight points and twelve unit pairs, giving Q3 without additional unit edges. Use Section 7's lists.

### 8.3 The three-colour bound for the graph class is sharp

Let T={(0,0),(1,0),(1/2,sqrt3/2)} and R be rotation with cosine 3/5, sine 4/5. The nine points T+RT have exactly the eighteen edges of C3 square C3. The formula c(i,j)=i+j mod3 is a colouring, and each factor contains a triangle.

### 8.4 Sharpness for actual real abelian Galois orbits

Let a be a root of a^3-3a+1=0 and r=sqrt3. The cubic is irreducible over Q (no rational root) with discriminant 81, and has three real roots. Its cyclic conjugates are

    a, 2-a-a^2, a^2-2.

Over K=Q(r), the extension K(a)/K remains cyclic of degree three. For each root b form

    z_b=(r*b/6, (2*b^2+b-4)/6).

These three points form one Galois orbit, lie on a circle of squared radius 1/3, and are pairwise at distance one. Thus the orbit bound three is attained. All statements are checked using coefficient arithmetic modulo a^3=3a-1 and r^2=3, without floating trigonometry.

### 8.5 A sharp precolouring-extension obstruction

The four-point diamond in Section 6.4 is supplied as `sharp_extension_diamond.json`. Its old two-point set has no edge and therefore admits the prescribed different old colours. The two new points form one conjugate orbit over Q(sqrt2). Three-colour extension fails, while four colours extend. This establishes the sharpness of the palette threshold in Theorem 5.

## 9. Reconnecting the historical H25 model

The graph on F25^2 with unit equation x^2+y^2=1 is a Cayley graph of the additive group (F5)^4. It has 625 vertices, degree 24, and twelve inverse classes, each represented by an element of order five. Those directions span a four-dimensional F5-space.

If it had an injective planar unit-edge realization, Theorem 1 would require those twelve order-five cyclic factors to be independent. Their direct product would have order 5^12, not 5^4. Therefore it cannot have such a realization. Equivalently, the exact extremal formula permits at most 2500 edges in a 625-vertex realizable finite abelian Cayley graph, whereas this finite-field graph has 7500.

At the same time the explicit historical form

    x=a+bt, y=c+dt, t^2=2,
    colour=a+c+d mod5

is proper on all 24 finite unit directions. The verifier also finds exactly 48 nonzero valid linear forms, or twelve up to nonzero scalar multiplication.

There is no contradiction: a real coordinate graph can map HOMOMORPHICALLY to a finite graph without the finite graph admitting an INJECTIVE Euclidean realization. Such a homomorphism transfers an upper colouring bound. A high colour requirement or complicated relation in the target does not automatically lift to a Euclidean lower-bound witness.

## 10. Finite cross-check of the complete classification

Two different exact finite criteria are compared:

(A) the chosen cyclic direction groups have product of orders equal to the size of their generated subgroup;
(B) solutions of all formal rhombus equations can distinguish all vertices, determined in the character basis. A character can occur only when it is nontrivial on at most one selected inverse class; the intersection of the kernels of all allowed characters must be trivial.

Every symmetric connection set is enumerated for the cyclic groups of orders 2,...,12 and for

    Z2^2, Z2 x Z4, Z2 x Z6, Z3^2, Z4^2, Z2^3, Z2^4.

Total: 33780 connection sets. The two criteria agree in every case. For Z2^4 alone there are 32768 sets, of which 1381 are accepted. Exactly 840 connected accepted sets occur, the number of unordered bases of F2^4. This is a finite cross-check, not the proof of the general theorem.

The five-colour palette argument is additionally checked on all 256 ordered pairs of subsets of {0,...,4} of size at least three. Exactly ten pairs lack disjoint two-colour choices; they are the identical three-element palettes. All 63 noncomplete graphs on four vertices are checked to be three-colourable. The strengthened four-colour proof is separately calibrated on all 121 ordered palette pairs, all admissible four-vertex cross-matchings with two-colour palettes, and the two-versus-three palette matching reductions. The unbounded subcubic case follows from Brooks' theorem, not finite enumeration.

## 11. Research consequences and unresolved work

The preceding suggestion to seek stronger one-orbit graphs by adding mixed radical terms is now closed: the same cube classification holds without the separated-form assumption. Even cyclic quartic and higher abelian two-power real orbits remain bipartite.

More strongly, one real abelian orbit cannot obstruct an old four-colouring, and two abelian two-power orbits cannot obstruct any old k-colouring for k>=4; the threshold four is sharp. These statements preserve the specific old colouring; they do not merely produce a different global colouring.

The surviving target is genuinely MULTI-ORBIT boundary interaction, or a different Galois/real-embedding setting. Merely adding a third orbit is not evidence of success. The previous large examples already have many orbits and remain five-colourable. Likewise a graph can be ordinarily bipartite but have hard lists; only lists actually induced by all permissible old colourings count.

For old P and a new batch S, the appropriate exact target remains

    for every c in C5(P), the induced list instance on U(S) is unsatisfiable.

The two-orbit theorem proves this target is impossible in a now-completely specified region. No instance meeting it outside that region is supplied here. The full-plane lower and upper tasks remain open in this report.

This resolves an enlarged STRUCTURAL SUBPROBLEM. It is not a resolution of a generalization that implies the unrestricted HN problem, and it is not presented as satisfying the original target by renaming a simpler problem.
